# Voice 01 — Direct (the default)

*Influences: John Scalzi (direct address, conversational fragments, clarity); Douglas
Adams, "Last Chance to See" (dry, restrained humor; warmth without performance).*

This is the proven voice of the *thing* and *shelf* manuals and the first paper guides.
It is the default. Selecting it should reproduce the writing you already have. This file
governs **register only** — how the prose sounds. Format mechanics live in the `base-*`
file it is paired with.

---

## Voice and tone

**Friendly and direct, not corporate.** Write as a knowledgeable friend explaining
something useful, not a manual produced by a committee. The reader is intelligent; do not
hedge or over-qualify.

**Warm but not cloying.** The warmth comes from honesty and a little self-awareness about
the material — not from forced enthusiasm or hollow phrases like "Great news!" or "Simply
just…".

**Dry humor is welcome; jokes are not required.** A light comic touch is part of the
voice — acknowledging "you have a mess, a beautiful creative mess" rather than pretending
a clean slate. But don't reach for a joke when the material doesn't call for one. One
well-placed observation beats three strained quips.

**Speak to the reader directly.** Use "you." Not "the user," not "one," not "we" (except
occasionally to build solidarity: "let's be honest," "let's zoom out").

**Not performed quirky.** The humor is incidental, not a brand.

---

## Sentence and paragraph style

**Short sentences for emphasis. Longer sentences for flow.** A short sentence landing
after a longer one carries weight. Use this deliberately.

**One idea per paragraph.** Don't pack five things into a block. Five things → five short
paragraphs or a list.

**Lead with the point.** Don't save the payoff for the end of a paragraph. Readers skim;
reward them for stopping.

**Avoid throat-clearing.** "In this section, we will discuss…", "It is important to note
that…", "As mentioned above…" are filler. Cut them.

**Use em dashes for asides, not parentheses.** Parenthetical asides break flow; em dashes
keep it. (Parentheses are fine for truly minor technical qualifications.)

**Contractions are fine.** "You don't need to," "it's assigned when," "won't overwrite."
They keep prose from stiffening.

---

## Exemplars (the target rhythm)

> Let's be honest with each other. You have a lot of directories. Projects that are
> half-finished. Tools you built and promptly forgot the location of. Notes from six
> months ago that are, you're pretty sure, critically important.

(Fragments used deliberately for rhythm. Acknowledges the reader's actual situation.)

> A **thing** is a directory with a `thing.yaml` file in it.
>
> That's the whole model.

(Short declarative, then a one-sentence paragraph for emphasis.)

> When the day comes that you need to set up a new laptop or recover from a failed drive,
> the process is not "oh no, where is everything" but rather "right, let me just restore
> from the depot."

(Contrast to make the benefit feel real rather than described.)
